\section{Motivation and Previous Work}

\paragraph{Pseudorandom Functions.}
A pseudorandom function (PRF) \cite{GolGolMic86} is a deterministic algorithm that takes as input 
a secret key $k$ and a point $x$ to produce an output $y$ that looks random in the sense that 
no efficient adversary, modeled by a probabilistic polynomial time (ppt.) Turing machine 
can distringuish the output $y$ from a random bitstring $r$ of the same length.
This must hold even if the adversary has access to a polynoial number of point evaluations 
$(x,y)$ before distinguishing the output.

\paragraph{Verifiable Functions.} 
A verifiable function (VF) is a public-key primitive that allows the holder of a secret key $\sk$
to evaluate points $x$ to obtain image-proof pairs $(y,\pi)$ such that any party holding the public key
$\pk$ can verify that $y$ was indeed computed correctly from $x$ if given the proof $\pi$.
We require correctness and unique provability from the verification which state that 
correctly generated image-proof pairs $(y,\pi)$ always (or except with negligible probability) is accepted by the 
verification algorithm and that at most one image can be proven respectively.

In this work we primarily study extensions of VRs, primarily the verifiable random function (VRF) introduced by 
Micali, Rabin and Vadhan in \cite{FOCS:MicRabVad99} that additionally requires the outputs $y$ to be 
pseudorandom to efficient adversaries without access to the corresponding proof.
Similar to the pseudorandomness definition for PRFs we require that no efficient adversary 
can distinguish the images $y$ from random outputs $r$, even if the adversary gets to see 
polynomially many evaluations of $x$ with their corresponding proof.
VRFs can be seen as a public-key analogoue of PRFs with verifiability requirements.

In practice VRFs are used to implement signatures with strong verifiability guarantees \cite{},
resettable zero-knowledge proofs \cite{}, lottery and e-cash systems \cite{} and they play a big 
role in distributed leader election protocols that runs many Proof-of-Stake blockchain systems \cite{}.

Additionally we study VFs satisfying weaker notions of pseudorandomness such as 
weak pseudorandomness and unpredictability which give rise to verifiable unpredictable functions 
(VUFs) \cite{} and weak verifiable random functions (wVRFs) \cite{} respectively. VUFs additionally require that 
the evaluation of the VF on a fresh evaluation point cannot be predicted by efficient adversaries 
except with negligible probability and wVRFs require that pseudorandomness holds, but only on 
uniformly random evaluation points $x$, instead of a point chosen by the adversary.

\paragraph{Robust Combiners.} 
In cryptography we often take a reductionist approach when constructing new cryptographic primitives,
in general constructions satisfying computational security properties rely on cryptographic hardness assumptions.
To hedge against the breakdown of cryptographic hardness assumptions, and thus the subsequence failure of the construction 
we may want to base the security of systems on multiple hardness assumptions.
Robust combiners \cite{} are constructions that combine $n$ candidate schemes, concrete instances of 
the same primitive, and implement a secure instance of that primitive if a large enough subset of those candidate is secure.
The notion of combiners has gained traction with the rise of post-quantum and hybrid-cryptography areas

Stronger version of robust combiners are robust black-box combiners \cite{} which are only given query access to the candidate
schemes as oracles, i.e. cannot inspect the Turing machine of the candidate scheme.
The motivation of a robust black-box combiner is to have a combiner that can implement a secure scheme 
only with access to sub-schemes with the underlying code of the candidate schemes are not accessible to the combiner.
We wish for the combiner to derive its security from the evaluation of the secure subset of oracles, 
not by making any modifications to the candidates or simply implementing a secure instance of the primitive himself.
For that reason we additionally require robust black-box combiners to implement the secure instance relative to a 
PSPACE complete oracle, that is guarantee the security against adversaries with access to a PSPACE oracle.

Robust black-box combiners have been considered for a variety of cryptographic primitives such as 
oblivious transfer \cite{}, non-interactive zero-knowledge proofs (NIZK) \cite{},
non-interactive witness-indistinguishable arguments (NIWI) \cite{}, indistinguishability obvuscation (iO) \cite{}
and signature schemes \cite{}. Additionally many folklore constructions exist for PRF, key exchange protocols \todo{add some}.
While there exist VRF constructions rely on PRFs and NIWIs, for both of which combiners have been studied,
black-box combiners for VRFs have, to the best of our knowledge, not been studied.

\section{PRF Combiners}\label{sec:intro_prf_combiners}
VRF can be considered as an extension of PRFs to the public-key setting with the additional requirements
of verifiability. Simple robust black-box PRF combiners exist. One combiner works by evaluating the evaluation point 
$x$ with every candidate scheme to obtain images $y_1, y_2$ and returning the XOR of the two images $y = y_1 \xor y_2$. 
Pseudorandomness of the combined scheme can be shown with a reductionist approach. Specifically we show that given an adversary 
against the combined scheme, we can construct an adversary against every candidate scheme running in the same time with the same advantage.
By contraposition, assuming that at least one candidate scheme is pseudorandom and there exists no efficient combiner with non-negligible 
advantage against the candidate we show that there exists no efficient adversary against the combined scheme with non-negligible advantage. 
We illustrate the construction of a robuts $(2;1)$ black-box PRF-combiner in \Cref{fig:prf_comb}.

\begin{figure}[hptb]
    \begin{pchstack}[center, space=1em]
        \procedure[linenumbering]
        {$f^{\O_1, \O_2}(k, x)$}
        {
            y_1 \gets \O_1(k,x)\\ 
            y_2 \gets \O_2(k,x)\\ 
            \pcreturn y_1 \xor y_2
        }
    \end{pchstack}
    \label{fig:prf_comb}
    \caption{Robust $(2;1)$ black-box PRF-combiner.}
\end{figure}

We outline the reduction and refer to the appendix for the formal proof. The reduction against candidate scheme $\O_i$
relies on the pseudorandomness oracle $\O_{i}^{prf}$ and simulates the other candidate $\O_{2-i}^{prf}$ himself to answer 
queries of the combiners adversary with $\O_{i}^{prf}(x) \oplus \O_{2-i}(k,x)$. The adversary against the combined scheme then 
guesses the hidden bit $b$, trying to distinguish the random world from the real world which the reduction then uses to 
guess its own hidden bit $b'$. If the hidden bit $b' = 0$ then the adversary $\A$ is simulated perfectly in the sense that 
the reduction wins the security experiment with the same advantage of $\A$ while for bit $b' = 1$ the answers to the queries 
of the adversary are still uniformly random bitstrings and therefore the reduction also wins with the same probability.

Note that the existance of a robust $(2;1)$ black-box PRF-combiner naturally leads to a robust $(n;1)$ black-box VRF-combiner 
for any natural number $n$ by iteratively applying the construction on the construction and a new candidate.
Since there exists a robust $(n;1)$ black-box PRF-combiner one could hope for a similar construction to work for VRFs. 
This is however not the case, as we will show in \Cref{thm:main} there exist no robust $(n; \lfloor n/2\rfloor)$ black-box combiner for VRFs.

\section{Our Contribution}

\paragraph{Robust Black-Box DAG Combiner.}
We introduce the notion of robust black-box DAG combiners, formally defined in 
\Cref{def:robust_blackbox_dag_combiner}, DAG combiners for short.
DAG combiners are black-box combiners which are defined by directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) for every 
implemented algorithm. The implemented algorithms are specified through computation that runs along the DAG 
starting from a designated source vertex and ending in a target vertex.
In the execution vertices correspond to queries to implementation functions of candidate oracles 
on an input computed from all preceding vertices by a ppt. Turing machine (without oracle access to the candidates).
DAG combiners make the structure of the black-box combiner more explicit 
by structuring it into interleaved oracle queries to the candidats and intermediate computation,
that we model with Turing machines without oracle access to the candidates. 
We illustrate this concept in \Cref{fig:prf_dag_comb} on the example of the simple robust black-box PRF-combiner in 
\Cref{fig:prf_comb}.

\begin{figure}[hptb]
    \begin{tikzpicture}
    \end{tikzpicture}
    \label{fig:prf_dag_comb}
    \caption{Robust $(2;1)$ Black-Box DAG PRF-combiner.}
\end{figure}

To connect DAG combiners and general black-box combiners we show that the notions are indeed equivalent,
that is, that for every general black-box combiner we can construct a DAG combiner 
that behaves identically in the sense that the algorithms defined by the combiners are equivalent.
We state this result formally in \Cref{thm:generality_dag_comb}.
The concept of a robust black-box DAG combiner allows us to reason over a more structured 
representation of robust black-box combiners, more similar to a circuit in structure
simplifying the proofs later on.  

\paragraph{Robust Black-Box VRF-Combiner.}

robust $(2;1)$ black-box VRF-combiners. We answer the question on whether there is a construction that 
combines two candidates, of which only at least one is secure, negatively. Specifically we show impossibility of 
robust $(2;1)$ black-box VRF-combiners in \Cref{lemma:warmup}.
