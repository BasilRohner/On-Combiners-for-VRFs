\section{Motivation and Previous Work}

\paragraph{Pseudorandom Functions.}
A pseudorandom function (PRF) \cite{GolGolMic86} is a deterministic algorithm that takes as input 
a secret key $k$ and a point $x$ to produce an output $y$ that is indistinguishable from 
a uniformly random bitstring of the same length. More precisely, no efficient adversary,
modeled by a probabilistic polynomial time Turing machine, can distinguish the outputs of a PRF from those 
of a truly random function, even after observing a polynomial number of input-output pairs.
PRFs are fundamental in modern cryptography and are used to construct, among other, 
symmetric encryption schemes \cite{KatLin14}, message authentication codes \cite{C:BelCanKra96}, key derivation functions \cite{C:Krawczyk10}.

\paragraph{Verifiable Random Functions.} 
A verifiable function (VF) is a public-key primitive that allows the holder of a secret key $\sk$
to evaluate an input $x$ and obtain an image-proof pair $(y,\pi)$, such that any party holding the corresponding public key
$\pk$ can verify that $y$ was correctly computed from $x$, if given the proof $\pi$.
We require two properties of the verification algorithm: \textit{correctness}, 
stating that honestly generated images are accepted by the verification algorithm (always, or except with negligible probability) and 
\textit{unique provability}, ensuring that for each input at most one image $y$ there exists a proof $\pi$ such that the 
verification algorithm accepts $(y,\pi)$.

In this work we study extensions of VFs, primarily the verifiable random function (VRF) introduced by 
Micali, Rabin and Vadhan \cite{FOCS:MicRabVad99}, which additionally require the images $y$ to be 
\textit{pseudorandom} to any efficient adversary without access to the corresponding proof $\pi$.
Analogously to the pseudorandomness definition for PRFs, we require that no efficient adversary 
can distinguish the images $y$ from images $y$ sampled uniformly at random from the image co-domain, 
even after observing polynomially many input-proof pairs for other evaluation points. 
VRFs can be seen as a public-key analogoue of PRFs with verifiability requirements.

In practice VRFs are used to implement signatures with strong verifiability guarantees \cite{C:GolOst92},
resettable zero-knowledge proofs \cite{C:MicRey01}, lottery systems \cite{RSA:MicRiv02a,cryptography4040037}, e-cash systems \cite{ACISP:AuSusMu07,PAIRING:BCKL09},
updatable zero-knowledge databases \cite{AC:Liskov05}, transaction escrow schemes \cite{EC:JarShm04} and they play a big 
role in Proof-of-Stake blockchains \cite{C:KRDO17,EC:DGKR18}, specifically in distributed leader election protocols \cite{EPRINT:BASV23b}.

Additionally we study VFs satisfying weaker notions of pseudorandomness such as 
weak pseudorandomness and unpredictability which give rise to verifiable unpredictable functions 
(VUFs) \cite{FOCS:MicRabVad99} and weak verifiable random functions (WVRFs) \cite{TCC:BGRV09} respectively. VUFs additionally require that 
the evaluation of the VF on a fresh evaluation point cannot be predicted by efficient adversaries 
except with negligible probability and WVRFs require that pseudorandomness holds, but only on 
uniformly random evaluation points $x$, instead of a point chosen by the adversary.

\paragraph{Robust Combiners.} 
In general, constructions staitsfying computational security properties rely on cryptographic hardness assumptions.
To hedge against the breakdown of such assumptions, and the subsequent failure of the constructions built on top of them,
it is desirable to base the security of a system on multiple hardness assumptions, such that security is compromised only if several assumptions fail simultaneously.
Robust $(n;k)$ combiners \cite{EC:HKNRR05} are constructions that combine $n$ candidate schemes, each a concrete instance of a given primitive,
to implement a secure instance of that same primitive provided that $k$ candidates remain secure.
The notion of combiners has gained traction with the rise of post-quantum and hybrid cryptography, where the goal is to build 
constructions based on both emerging quantum-resistant assumptions and well-tested classical ones, ensuring that security is preserved 
even if one class of assumptions proves vulnerable.
 
A more restricted notion are robust $(n;k)$ black-box combiners \cite{EC:HKNRR05},
which are only given oracle access to the $n$ candicates, i.e. they may query the candidates 
on inputs to obtain the output but cannot inspect their underlying code. 
The motivation behind robust black-box combiners is to ensure that the combiner derives its security soley
from evaluations of the underlying candidates, rather than by modifying them or independently implementing a secure instance of te primitive
from scratch. 

Robust black-box combiners have been considered for a variety of cryptographic primitives, such as 
oblivious transfer \cite{EC:HKNRR05}, non-interactive zero-knowledge proofs \cite{EPRINT:CMVX25},
indistinguishability obfuscation and witness encryption \cite{C:AJNSY16}. 
Additionally, folklore constructions exist for PRF, one-way functions and key agreement \cite{EC:HKNRR05}, symmetric encryption and public-key encryption.
Black-box combiners for VRFs have, to the best of our knowledge, not been studied.

\section{PRF Combiners}\label{sec:intro_prf_combiners}
VRFs can be viewed as a public-key version of PRFs with an additional verifiability requirement.
Simple robust black-box PRF combiners are known for PRFs. One such combiner evaluates the input 
$x$ under each candidate scheme to obtain images $y_1, y_2$ and returns their XOR $y = y_1 \xor y_2$.
We illustrate the construction in \Cref{fig:prf_comb}.

\begin{figure}[hptb]
    \begin{pchstack}[center, space=1em]
        \procedure[linenumbering]
        {$\alg{Eval}{PRF}^{\O_1, \O_2}(k, x)$}
        {
            y_1 \gets \O_1(k,x)\\ 
            y_2 \gets \O_2(k,x)\\ 
            \pcreturn y_1 \xor y_2
        }
    \end{pchstack}
    \label{fig:prf_comb}
    \caption{Robust $(2;1)$ Black-Box PRF-combiner initialized with candidate schemes $\O_1, \O_2$.}
\end{figure}
Pseudorandomness of the combined scheme follows from a standard reductionist argument:
given an adversary $\A$ against the combiner, we construct an adversary $\mathcal B$ against each individual candidate that runs in the same 
time as $\A$ and breaks the candidates pseudorandomness if $\A$ breaks the combiners pseudorandomness.
By contraposition, if at least one candidate is pseudorandom then the combiner is pseudorandom. 
We outline the reduction and refer to the \Cref{sec:app_robust_prf_comb} for a formal proof.

The reduction against candidate scheme $\O_i$ has access to the pseudorandomness oracle 
$\O_{\text{eval}}$ of the pseudorandomness security experiment against $\O_i$ and internally simulates the 
other candidate $\O_{2-i}$, answering queries from $\A$ with $\O_{\text{eval}}(x) \xor \O_{2-i}(k,x)$.
Note that the $\mathcal B$ makes exactly as many queries to $\O_{\text{eval}}$ as $\A$ does during its simulation, 
so $\mathcal B$ is efficient whenever $\A$ is.
The adversary $\A$ against the combined scheme outputs a guess $b'$ for the hidden bit $b$ sampled by the challenger u.a.r., attempting to distinguish 
the real world from the random world. The reduction simply forwards this guess to its own challenger.
If $b = 0$, the adversary $\A$ is simulated perfectly, as it receives images computed exactly as they would be in the 
pseudorandomness security experiment against the combiner. If $b = 1$ then $\O_{\text{eval}}(x)$ returns images sampled u.a.r. 
from the set of bitstrings with appropriate length. Since $\O_{\text{eval}}(x)$ is uniformly distributed, $\O_{\text{eval}}(x) \xor \O_{2-i}(k,x)$
is uniformly distributed as well. Since $\A$ is perfectly simulated in both worlds
$\mathcal B$ wins the pseudorandomness experiment with the same advantage as $\A$. 

The existence of a robust $(2;1)$ black-box PRF-combiner naturally leads to a robust $(n;1)$ black-box PRF combiner
for any $n \in \N^+$, by iteratively applying the pairwise construction with a fresh candidate at each step. 
Since there exist simple robust $(n;1)$ black-box PRF-combiner one might hope that an analogous approach extends to VRFs. This turns out 
not to be the case as we show in \Cref{thm:impossibility_21_comb} that there exists no robust $(2;1)$ black-box VRF-combiner. 

\section{Our Contribution}

\paragraph{Robust Black-Box DAG Combiner.}
We introduce the notion of robust black-box DAG combiners, formally defined in 
\Cref{def:robust_blackbox_dag_combiner}. A robust black-box DAG combiner is a black-box combiner whose implemented 
algorithms are each specified by a directed acyclic graph (DAG) $\G$. Execution of the algorithm proceeds along the DAG starting 
from a designated source vertex $s$ and ending in a designated sink vertex $t$.
At each intermediate vertex $v \in V(\G)$, a ppt. Turing machine, without access to any oracles, computes an a query input 
$\q_v$ from the outputs of all preceding vertices, which is subsequently queried to an oracle algorithm of a candidate specified 
by a vertex labeling $\ell(v)$ to obtain an output $\x_v$.
DAG combiners make the structure of the black-box combiner more explicit 
by decomposing it into interleaved oracle queries to the candidates and intermediate (oracle independent)
computation steps.
We illustrate the concept of a robust black-box DAG combiner in \Cref{fig:prf_dag_comb} using the robust black-box PRF combiner from \Cref{fig:prf_comb} as an example.

We relate robust black-box DAG combiners with general robust black-box combiners by showing that the two notions are 
equivalent in the sense that for every general robust black-box combiner there exists 
a robust black-box DAG combiner whose algorithms behave identically (the converse holds definitionally).
We state this result formally in \Cref{thm:generality_dag_comb}. The robust black-box DAG combiner 
framrwork provides a more structured representation of robust black-box combiners which simplifies our proof for 
\Cref{thm:impossibility_21_comb}.

\paragraph{Robust Black-Box VRF-Combiners.}
With the robust black-box DAG combiner framework in place, we consider the simplest robust black-box VRF-combiner.
Namely, the robust $(2;1)$ black-box VRF-combiner, where only two candidates are combined and security of the combined 
scheme is required to hold if at least one of the candidates is secure. We prove that no such combiner exists in 
\Cref{thm:impossibility_21_comb}.
As we can use a robust $(n;\lfloor n/2\rfloor)$ black-box VRF-combiner to construct a robust $(2;1)$ black-box combiner 
we extend the impossibility of a robust $(2;1)$ black-box VRF-combiner in \Cref{thm:impossibility_21_comb} to the impossibility of an infinite family of robust $(n;\lfloor n/2\rfloor)$ black-box combiners
for all $n \in \N$ in \Cref{lemma:nhalf}. 

Finally, we investigate robust black-box combiners under strengthened assumptions on the candidate schemes or relaxed requirements on the combiner 
construction. We show that if all candidate schemes are assumed to be uniquely provable and perfectly correct, then 
a simple robust $(n;1)$ black-box VRF-combiner exists.
This construction extends the robust $(n;1)$ black-box PRF-combiner presented in \Cref{fig:prf_comb}.
